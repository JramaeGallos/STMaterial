Papalitan ba ako ng AI?
Tasks, not jobs.

You've probably seen reels saying AI will wipe out jobs, so why bother studying? Before you pick your college course, let's check claims like that against what researchers have actually found.

↓ Scroll to see how AI really changes work

1 · The big idea

A job is a bundle of tasks

The International Labour Organization (ILO) doesn't just ask "Will AI take this job?" Its researchers scored 29,753 individual work tasks to see which ones generative AI could potentially do, then rated each occupation based on its tasks (Gmyrek et al., 2025).

Think of a job like a playlist. AI can remix or skip some tracks, but there's still a playlist. The ILO puts it this way: since most occupations include tasks that need human input, transformation of jobs is the most likely impact (Gmyrek et al., 2025).

3 · Science in progress

Wait, why don't the numbers match?

Good question! The two studies measured different things, so their results differ.

IMF

Used a broad AI exposure measure, not only chatbots. It also rated whether AI is more likely to complement (support) workers or put their tasks at risk of replacement (Cucio & Hennig, 2025).

Result: 36% of jobs highly exposed (IMF, 2024).

vs

ILO

Looked only at generative AI, the kind that writes text and makes images. It used a global index built from 29,753 scored tasks (Gmyrek et al., 2025).

Result: 27.7% of jobs exposed; 3.6% in the highest group (Huynh, 2026).

What both agree on: exposure is not the same as job loss. The IMF found that more than half of highly exposed jobs are ones where AI is more likely to support than replace tasks (IMF, 2024). The ILO says exposure "does not necessarily equate to job loss" (Huynh, 2026, p. 3).

The honest limits: the ILO notes its global index may not fully reflect local Philippine conditions (Huynh, 2026, p. 3, note 7). Whether exposure leads to job losses also depends on whether employers adopt the technology and whether workers get chances to learn to work with it (Gmyrek et al., 2025).

4 · Fairness check

AI's impact isn't equal

Here's the ethics part: AI exposure doesn't land evenly on everyone. Some groups carry more of it.

2×

Women are more exposed

40.3% of jobs held by women are exposed to generative AI, compared with 19.3% for men. Women make up about three in five clerical support and service and sales workers (Huynh, 2026, p. 5).

BPO

The most exposed sector

The IMF identifies business process outsourcing as the Philippines' most AI-exposed sector. It classifies BPO workers as highly exposed with low complementarity, the group more at risk of displacement (Cucio & Hennig, 2025).

42.4%

Metro Manila jobs exposed

About two in five NCR jobs (2.6 million) are exposed, reflecting its many IT-BPM and other professional service jobs. Central Luzon and CALABARZON are above 30% (Huynh, 2026, pp. 1, 5–6).

1st job

The entry-level ladder

At a 2026 PIDS webinar, an expert warned that AI may weaken the traditional path through which workers gain experience and move up. That's the path you'd start on (PIDS, 2026).

5 · Behind the screen

Meet the humans in the loop

AI looks automatic, but people are part of the system. Some train it, some check it, and some set the rules for it. Their stories show that AI at work is also a fairness question.

🏷️
1 · The trainers

The people who teach AI

AI learns from data that people label by hand. Washington Post reporters interviewed Filipino freelancers doing this work on Remotasks, a platform run by the AI company Scale AI. All but two said their pay had been delayed, reduced or cancelled after finishing tasks. The company said payment problems were very rare (Tan & Cabato, 2023).

Think about it: Who should make sure online AI workers are paid fairly: the platform, the government, or both?

🔍
2 · The checkers

The people who double-check AI

AI output still needs a human eye. In a Department of Labor and Employment study, workers said their workload increased after AI was adopted. Some now spend extra time verifying AI-generated outputs and checking for errors (PIDS, 2026).

Think about it: If AI saves time but adds checking work, is the job easier, or just different?

⚖️
3 · The rule-makers

The people who decide who benefits

Technology alone doesn't decide who gains. The ILO calls for government, employers and workers to work together so that AI's benefits are inclusive and workers, especially youth, are protected and prepared (Huynh, 2026, p. 1). The IMF recommends stronger social protection, including unemployment insurance and reskilling programs (Cucio & Hennig, 2025).

Think about it: What should your school, your future employer and the government each do?

AI's impact on work isn't only about machines. It also depends on human choices, and that includes yours.

Skills worth building now, whatever your strand

🔍 Checking AI's work

Some Filipino workers now spend extra time verifying AI outputs and checking for errors (PIDS, 2026). Practice fact-checking now.

🤝 Soft skills

Communication, critical thinking and creativity "may become even more valuable," according to an expert at a PIDS forum (PIDS, 2026).

🧠 Skills you can show

Companies are shifting from degree-based hiring toward skills-based recruitment (PIDS, 2026). Build a portfolio of what you can actually do.

🌱 Keep learning

Employers surveyed worldwide expect 170 million jobs to be created and 92 million displaced by 2030 (World Economic Forum, 2025, p. 5). The ILO urges preparing youth for changing job profiles (Huynh, 2026, p. 1).

Using AI at school, the smart way

  • Use it to understand, not to skip the thinking.
  • Verify every fact it gives you against a real source, like this page does.
  • Declare when you used AI, following your school's rules.
  • Protect your privacy: don't paste personal info into chatbots.

For parents & teachers: start the conversation

Instead of asking "Which course is safe from AI?", try asking: "Which tasks in this job do you enjoy, and how could AI help with the rest?" Then look up the job's ILO exposure level together in Section 1.
